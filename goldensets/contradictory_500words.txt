Fiscal policy is government use of spending and taxation to influence the economy. Keynesians advocate deficit spending during recessions to stimulate demand and create jobs. Government spending has a multiplier effect that boosts economic growth.

However, the crowding-out effect occurs when government borrowing raises interest rates and reduces private investment. This negates the multiplier effect. Higher government spending today means higher taxes tomorrow, reducing future consumption.

Supply-side economics argues tax cuts increase incentives to work, save, and invest. Lower taxes on capital and labor increase productivity. The Laffer Curve shows tax revenue initially rises with tax rates, then falls after reaching an optimal point.

Modern Monetary Theory claims governments that issue their own currency can never run out of money. They can spend freely until inflation becomes a problem. Inflation is the real constraint, not debt.

Austrian economics warns that central bank intervention causes malinvestment and business cycles. Interest rates should be market-determined, not manipulated. Fractional reserve banking is inherently unstable.

Deficit hawks insist national debt is a burden on future generations. Interest payments consume tax revenues that could fund services. Debt-to-GDP ratios above 90% reduce growth.

Each school of thought claims empirical support. Each has prominent economists defending it. Policy debates remain unresolved.
