**Layer 1: The Sticky Analogy**

Imagine you are running a sidewalk lemonade stand during a scorching summer heatwave. A thirsty customer asks for a cup, but you raise the price from $1 to $2.

Because the sun is beating down and there is no other stand nearby, they grumble but buy it anyway. Their demand for relief is **inelastic** because they have no choice but to pay.

Now imagine it is a mild spring day with three other stands within sight. If you raise the price to $2, they walk away to the cheaper option. Their demand is **elastic** because substitutes are readily available.

**Layer 2: The Translation**

Price elasticity of demand quantifies how much the quantity demanded changes relative to a change in price, holding other factors constant. **The core concept is that responsiveness depends on available substitutes and necessity.**

Formally, it is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. If the absolute value exceeds one, demand is elastic; if below one, it is inelastic.

There is a direct tension between price changes and total revenue. Raising prices increases revenue only if demand is inelastic, but decreases revenue if demand is elastic.

**Layer 3: The Exam Crib Sheet**

**Price Elasticity Formula:** $E_d = \frac{\% \Delta Q_d}{\% \Delta P} = \frac{\Delta Q_d / Q_{d,avg}}{\Delta P / P_{avg}}$. The midpoint method uses averages for $Q$ and $P$ to ensure calculation consistency regardless of direction.

**Revenue Test Logic:** If Price $\uparrow$ and Total Revenue $\downarrow$, demand is elastic ($|E_d| > 1$). If Price $\uparrow$ and Total Revenue $\uparrow$, demand is inelastic ($|E_d| < 1$).

**Why Use Midpoint?** It prevents asymmetrical results where calculating elasticity from $P_1$ to $P_2$ yields a different value than $P_2$ to $P_1$.

**Real-World Scenarios:**
1. **Taxation:** Governments tax inelastic goods like gasoline or insulin because consumption doesn't drop significantly, ensuring stable tax revenue.
2. **Pricing Strategy:** Airlines raise prices on last-minute tickets (inelastic business travelers) but discount early bookings (elastic leisure travelers with substitutes).
3. **Long-Run Adjustments:** Electricity demand is inelastic short-term (you need heat) but elastic long-term (you buy better insulation).

**Gotcha Points:**
*   Always take the absolute value $|E_d|$ when classifying elasticity, as demand curves slope downward making the raw number negative.
*   Remember that **time horizon** is a determinant; demand is almost always more elastic in the long run as consumers find alternatives.
