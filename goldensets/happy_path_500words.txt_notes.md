**Layer 1: The Sticky Analogy**

Imagine you are buying coffee versus buying life-saving medication when prices suddenly spike. If the coffee shop raises prices by 10%, you might simply switch to tea or make it at home, causing their total sales to plummet despite higher prices. This reaction happens because coffee has many easy substitutes and is not strictly necessary for survival today.

In contrast, if the price of your essential medication rises by 10%, you will likely continue buying the same amount because your health depends on it. Consequently, the pharmacy sees an increase in total revenue because your demand is rigid and unresponsive to the price hike.

**The core lesson is that consumer sensitivity to price depends entirely on whether a product is a replaceable luxury or an unavoidable necessity.**

**Layer 2: The Translation**

Economic analysts use Price Elasticity of Demand (Ed) to quantify how much the quantity demanded shifts in response to a price change. Technically, this is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price, holding all other factors constant.

When |Ed| > 1, demand is elastic, meaning consumers are highly responsive, often because substitutes are available or the item is a luxury. When |Ed| < 1, demand is inelastic, meaning consumers are unresponsive, typical for necessities with few substitutes.

A critical trade-off exists between price changes and total revenue: raising prices increases revenue for inelastic goods but decreases it for elastic goods. The midpoint method is used to calculate elasticity to ensure consistent results regardless of the direction of the price change.

**Total revenue moves in the same direction as price for inelastic demand, but in the opposite direction for elastic demand.**

**Layer 3: The Exam Crib Sheet**

The standard formula for Price Elasticity of Demand using the midpoint method is:
Ed = [(Q2-Q1)/((Q1+Q2)/2)] / [(P2-P1)/((P1+P2)/2)]

Other key elasticities include Cross-price elasticity (Exy = %ΔQx / %ΔPy) for substitutes/complements and Income elasticity (Ey = %ΔQd / %ΔI) for normal/inferior goods.

The midpoint method is preferred because it yields the same elasticity value whether prices rise or fall, avoiding the uneven base problem inherent in simple percentage changes. Determinants of elasticity include substitute availability, necessity status, income proportion, and time horizon.

Scenario 1: A government taxes gasoline (inelastic) to generate stable revenue because consumers cannot easily stop driving. Scenario 2: A luxury brand raises prices, expecting revenue to drop (elastic) but accepting lower volume for higher margin per unit. Scenario 3: Utility companies raise rates, knowing revenue will rise (inelastic) because households have no short-term alternatives.

**Gotcha 1: Always take the absolute value |Ed| when classifying elasticity types for simple price changes, though cross-price elasticity signs matter for substitutes (+) vs complements (-).** **Gotcha 2: Confusing the direction of the Total Revenue Test; remember that for elastic demand, Price and Revenue move in opposite directions.**
