Title: Price Elasticity of Demand — Theory and Applications

Price elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price. It is defined as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price, holding all other factors constant (ceteris paribus).

The formula is:
Ed = (%ΔQd) / (%ΔP) = (ΔQd/Qd_avg) / (ΔP/P_avg)

Demand is elastic when |Ed| > 1, meaning consumers are highly responsive to price changes. Luxury goods and products with many substitutes tend to be elastic. A 10% price increase might cause a 25% quantity drop in this case, making total revenue decrease when prices rise.

Demand is inelastic when |Ed| < 1, meaning consumers are relatively unresponsive to price changes. Necessities like insulin, gasoline, and utilities tend to be inelastic. A 10% price increase might cause only a 3% quantity drop, making total revenue increase when prices rise.

Unit elastic occurs when |Ed| = 1, where total revenue remains unchanged by price adjustments.

The key determinants of price elasticity include: availability of substitutes (more substitutes = more elastic), necessity vs. luxury (necessities are inelastic), proportion of income spent (higher proportion = more elastic), and time horizon (demand is more elastic in the long run as consumers find alternatives).

Total revenue test: If price increases and total revenue increases, demand is inelastic. If price increases and total revenue decreases, demand is elastic. This has direct pricing implications for businesses.

The midpoint method ensures consistent elasticity calculations regardless of direction:
Ed = (Q2-Q1)/((Q1+Q2)/2) ÷ (P2-P1)/((P1+P2)/2)

Cross-price elasticity of demand measures how quantity demanded of good X responds to price changes in good Y:
Exy = (%ΔQx) / (%ΔPy)

Income elasticity of demand measures how quantity demanded responds to changes in consumer income:
Ey = (%ΔQd) / (%ΔI)

These elasticity concepts are crucial for tax incidence analysis, pricing strategy, and understanding how market shocks affect consumer behavior. Governments consider elasticity when taxing goods — inelastic goods are easier to tax because quantity demanded doesn't drop significantly, making them more reliable revenue sources.
